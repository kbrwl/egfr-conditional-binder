Human vs mouse EGFR: what matches, what doesn't, and where to aim

A working document for the conditional binder design. Everything below is computed from the two official sequence records, not recalled from memory.

First: how to read the shorthand

Protein people write a single difference as three things squashed together. Here is Q390R pulled apart:

Qthe letter in the human protein — Q means glutamine
390the position — the 390th link in the chain, counting from one end
Rthe letter in the mouse protein — R means arginine

So Q390R reads: "at position 390, humans have glutamine where mice have arginine." Each of the 20 amino acids has an agreed single letter. They are not always the first letter of the name (R for arginine, K for lysine, W for tryptophan), which is why the notation looks arbitrary until you have the table. What the alignment shows

The two proteins are close relatives but not identical twins. Across the whole outside portion they agree at roughly 87 positions out of every 100. Inside domain III — the sub-region the existing cancer drugs grab — they agree at about 91 out of 100.

The useful part isn't the percentage. It's that the disagreements are lumpy. Almost all of them sit in the first half of domain III. Past position 415 there are only two differences in the next fifty-two positions.

The candidate landing zone: 415 to 466

Fifty-two consecutive positions containing exactly one difference, at position 442, where human has serine and mouse has glycine. Both are among the smallest amino acids, so the shape barely changes. For practical purposes this whole block is the same protein surface in both species.

Two features make it attractive beyond just being long:

It carries six negatively charged residues

D416, E421, E424, E455, D458 and D460. D is aspartic acid and E is glutamic acid; both carry a negative charge at the acidity levels we care about, and all six are identical in mouse.

These are the anchors for the pH trick. Your binder puts a histidine across from one of them. Histidine is the one amino acid that changes charge in the window between healthy tissue and tumour tissue: neutral at pH 7.4, positive at around pH 6.5. Neutral histidine and a negative aspartic acid ignore each other. Positive histidine and a negative aspartic acid pull together. That is the entire on/off switch, and six anchor points is generous raw material.

The target already has two histidines here: H418 and H433

Both identical in mouse. They also turn positive in the tumour, and that cuts both ways. Put a positive residue on your binder facing them and the pair will shove apart exactly when you want them to grip. Put a negative residue there instead and you gain extra grip for free. So these two are not a problem — they are a second, opposite-facing lever, as long as you point them deliberately.

Limit of this analysis. Everything above comes from comparing letters. Letters tell you what each residue is, not which direction it points. Domain III folds into a spiral-staircase shape, so a residue can sit buried in the core with its neighbour sticking out into open water. Some of those six negatively charged residues are certainly buried and useless to us. Resolving that needs the three-dimensional coordinates, which is the next step below.

Why cetuximab fails on mouse EGFR

Cetuximab (sold as Erbitux) is an approved antibody for colorectal and head-and-neck cancer. An antibody is a Y-shaped protein the immune system produces; the two tips of the Y are the gripping surfaces. Cetuximab's tips clamp onto domain III of human EGFR, covering the patch that the natural growth signal needs to land on. Signal blocked, tumour growth slowed. Panitumumab (Vectibix) is a second approved antibody that grabs an overlapping patch.

Cetuximab does not bind mouse EGFR at all. The reason is structural and simple: the specific residues its tips touch are among the ones that differ between the species. The antibody was raised against the human surface and fits it like a cast. Change a few of the bumps and the cast no longer seats.

This is a real practical problem, not a trivia point. When researchers want to test an EGFR drug in mice before going near humans, they cannot inject the human antibody — it would do nothing. They have to build a separate mouse-specific surrogate molecule, which costs time and money and means the mouse results are about a different molecule than the one heading to patients. The competition asks for one binder that works in both species precisely to remove that step.

The region cetuximab covers sits in the second half of domain III, overlapping the growth-signal binding surface. Several of our computed differences fall in or near that window — including Q390R, E412D, R414W and K467R — which is consistent with the species failure.

Marked as unverified. The precise list of residues cetuximab touches should be computed from the published structure of the antibody bound to EGFR rather than taken from anyone's memory, including mine. That calculation is step 2 below. Until it runs, treat the four residues named above as a plausible explanation, not an established one.

Note the useful consequence either way: our candidate block, 415 to 466, sits right next to the drug-binding region without being identical to it. Close enough that a binder landing there should block the growth signal the same way. Different enough that we are not simply rebuilding cetuximab, which the competition rules forbid.

Next steps

Get the three-dimensional structure

Download the published coordinates of cetuximab gripping EGFR domain III (archive entry 1YY9). A structure file is a plain text table listing the x, y and z position of every atom, measured experimentally. This is free and public.

Work out which residues are actually reachable

For each position in 415–466, calculate how much of it is exposed to the surrounding water. The standard method rolls a water-sized ball over the protein and measures how much of each residue it can touch. High number means a binder can reach it; near zero means it is buried and irrelevant to us. This prunes our six negatively charged anchors down to the ones that exist on the surface.

Compute cetuximab's true footprint

List every EGFR residue with an atom sitting within about 4.5 ångströms of an antibody atom — roughly the distance at which two atoms are considered in contact. That gives the real contact set, and confirms or kills the explanation above.

Check the surviving residues form a cluster

A binder is a single small object and can only touch one patch. If the exposed anchors turn out to be scattered across opposite faces of the fold, the epitope is not real and we fall back to the next-longest conserved run. They need to sit within roughly 25 ångströms of each other.

Generate binder candidates against that patch

Feed the confirmed patch into a binder-design pipeline, which proposes candidate sequences shaped to fit it. Generate a few hundred, then filter on how confidently a structure-prediction model places them against the target.

Engineer the pH switch

Take the surviving candidates and systematically swap contact residues to histidine, keeping the versions where a histidine lands close to one of the confirmed negatively charged anchors, and where the fold still holds together.

Novelty check, rank, submit

Confirm no sequence resembles a known binder closely enough to break the de-novo rule, rank the survivors, and submit the top twenty with written reasoning.
